Abstract
Background Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have brought AI resurrection, the digital reconstruction of the 
deceased closer to reality. Understanding the psychological factors associated with acceptance of this technology is 
essential for informing its ethical and educational implications.
Methods This cross–sectional study investigated Chinese college students’ acceptance of AI resurrection and 
examined demographic, death-related background factors, and AI-related predictors. A total of 990 valid responses 
were collected through an online self-administered questionnaire (Cronbach’s α = 0.799). Spearman’s correlation and 
multinomial logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between variables and acceptance 
levels across five hypothetical scenarios.
Results Higher AI engagement and stronger perceived harm predictability were positively associated with 
acceptance of AI resurrection technologies, whereas fear of AI demonstrated scenario–dependent effects. Participants 
who had experienced the death of loved ones or studied medicine showed greater acceptance. Nearly half of the 
respondents (47.9%) believed that both the deceased’s and family’s wishes should be considered in resurrection 
decisions.
Conclusions Cognitive and affective factors were associated with individuals’ acceptance of AI resurrection 
technologies. These findings contribute to understanding human-AI interaction in existential contexts and emphasize 
the importance of consent and data protection in future applications.

Background
As artificial intelligence (AI) increasingly intersects with 
fundamental aspects of human life and death, under -
standing its psychological and ethical implications has 
become a critical concern. AI has rapidly advanced to a 
stage where technologies can replicate human voices, 
images, and personalities from digital traces, enabling 
what has recently been termed AI resurrection. This con -
cept refers to the digital reconstruction of a deceased 
individual through AI systems capable of generating 
interactive simulations or chatbots [ 1, 2]. While once 
confined to speculative fiction, the growing capabilities 
of generative AI systems such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek 
have transformed these ideas into feasible technological 
possibilities [ 3, 4]. These developments raise profound 
psychological questions about how humans respond to 
technologies that blur the boundary between the living 
and the dead. Existing studies have examined the ethi -
cal and legal implications of posthumous data use and 
digital personhood [ 5–7]. However, empirical research 
on individuals’ psychological acceptance of AI resurrec -
tion remains scarce. Understanding public perceptions 
of such technologies is essential, as social acceptance 
strongly predicts adoption and compliance behaviours 
toward emerging AI systems [8, 9].
University students represent a particularly relevant 
demographic for investigating these issues. They are early 
adopters of digital technology, frequently engage with 
AI in educational and personal contexts, and will likely 
shape the ethical and societal norms surrounding future 
AI use [ 10, 11]. Moreover, cultural beliefs about death 
and continuity of the self, particularly those informed 
by Confucian and Daoist traditions, may inform emo -
tional and moral evaluations of digital resurrection 
technologies [ 2, 12]. These cultural frameworks empha -
size relationality, reverence for ancestors, and the moral 
significance of remembrance, providing a cultural lens 
through which participants may interpret AI-mediated 
representations of the dead. Accordingly, the discussion 
of cultural traditions in this study reflects an interpretive 
context rather than direct statistical testing.
Unlike conventional AI applications that primarily 
support efficiency or decision-making, AI resurrection 
involves deeply personal and existential domains, includ -
ing mortality, identity continuity, and posthumous auton-
omy. These characteristics raise questions about whether 
traditional technology acceptance frameworks remain 
applicable in such contexts. Conceptually, even in exis -
tentially sensitive contexts, judgments of acceptability 
involve cognitive evaluation and emotional response to 
an AI system.
From this perspective, the Technology Acceptance 
Model (TAM) [ 8] and the Human–AI Trust Frame -
work [ 13] remain theoretically relevant, as they offer 
complementary lenses for understanding how individuals 
assess, trust, and emotionally react to AI technologies in 
morally charged contexts. TAM provides a foundation for 
examining how cognitive evaluations, such as familiarity, 
perceived ease of interaction, and anticipated usefulness, 
shape acceptance. In the context of AI resurrection, these 
evaluations extend beyond instrumental utility to include 
judgments about psychological comfort and perceived 
appropriateness of interacting with a posthumous digital 
entity.
At the same time, the Human–AI Trust Framework is 
particularly pertinent to AI resurrection because trust-
related beliefs, such as predictability, transparency, and 
controllability, become central when AI systems oper -
ate in ethically sensitive domains. When AI technologies 
engage with death and identity, uncertainty regarding 
potential harm or misuse may undermine trust and 
amplify emotional resistance.
Therefore, integrating TAM with trust-based perspec -
tives allows for a more nuanced understanding of how 
cognitive familiarity, perceived predictability, and affec -
tive responses jointly shape acceptance of AI resurrec -
tion. These frameworks are employed as psychologically 
grounded models for examining how complex moral con-
cerns are translated into acceptance judgments. By situ -
ating these frameworks within the Chinese socio-cultural 
context and considering death-related beliefs and AI-
related affect (e.g., AI fear), this study aims to extend our 
understanding of how AI resurrection might be evalu -
ated within a broader cultural and moral context.
The psychological acceptance of AI technologies
Building on the above theoretical positioning, psycholog -
ical acceptance of technology has been extensively exam -
ined through the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), 
which posits that behavioural intention is determined by 
cognitive evaluations such as perceived usefulness and 
perceived ease of use [8]. TAM and its extensions, such as 
the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 
[9], have been applied across various contexts, including 
healthcare [14], education [15], and social media [16].
Within this literature, TAM primarily conceptualizes 
acceptance as a cognitive evaluation process, emphasiz -
ing individuals’ assessments of usefulness, ease of inter -
action, and familiarity with technology. Traditional TAM 
frameworks mainly emphasize cognitive rather than 
emotional processes. Subsequent research suggests that 
affective factors, including trust, anxiety, and fear, play 
critical roles in shaping user attitudes toward AI [ 13, 
17]. In particular, trust in AI depends on users’ percep -
tions of predictability, transparency, and controllabil -
ity of AI behaviour [ 18, 19]. Conversely, AI-related fear 
or anxiety may be associated with heightened perceived 
risks and moral unease and lower acceptance [ 20, 21].

Accordingly, integrating cognitive and affective dimen -
sions has become increasingly important for understand -
ing acceptance of AI systems that involve uncertainty and 
ethical sensitivity.
The role of cognitive engagement and harm predictability
AI engagement, defined as the degree of familiarity, 
understanding, and frequency of use of AI tools, reflects 
the cognitive component of interaction. Higher engage -
ment enhances perceived ease of use and reduces uncer -
tainty, which is associated with higher acceptance [ 14, 
21]. Research in educational psychology also shows that 
repeated exposure to AI systems increases self-efficacy 
and technology readiness [22, 23].
A related construct, AI harm predictability, refers to 
users’ belief that AI behaviour is transparent and con -
trollable, which are concepts central to the Human-AI 
Trust Framework [13]. When people perceive AI systems 
as predictable and aligned with human values, they are 
more likely to trust and accept them [ 13, 18, 21]. Trans-
parency in AI decision-making can mitigate uncertainty 
and enhance psychological safety [ 24, 25]. These findings 
suggest that cognitive variables, namely engagement and 
perceived predictability, may serve as key facilitators of 
acceptance toward ethically complex AI applications.
The role of AI fear
In contrast, emotional responses such as AI fear can 
inhibit acceptance. Fear of AI encompasses apprehension 
about potential harm, ethical misuse, or loss of human 
control [ 20]. This affective response is often rooted in 
perceived threats to identity, autonomy, or existential 
security [ 26, 27]. From a psychological perspective, fear 
may be linked to avoidance tendencies and moral resis -
tance, and has been associated with lower acceptance 
of technologies perceived as invasive or dehumanizing. 
Studies have shown that individuals experiencing higher 
AI-related fear are less likely to adopt AI-driven systems 
regardless of their functionality [ 28, 29]. In contexts 
involving mortality or posthumous data, fear may inten -
sify because of perceived violations of human dignity and 
spiritual boundaries [2, 30]. Therefore, examining AI fear 
alongside cognitive variables is crucial for understanding 
affective resistance toward AI resurrection.
Taken together, this study conceptualizes cognitive 
and affective factors as parallel yet interacting pathways 
influencing acceptance of AI resurrection. Cognitive 
evaluations, such as AI engagement and perceived harm 
predictability, reflect individuals’ rational assessments 
of familiarity, controllability, and trustworthiness of AI 
systems. In parallel, affective responses, particularly AI-
related fear, capture emotional reactions rooted in ethical 
concern, existential unease, and perceived moral risk.
Rather than assuming a fixed hierarchical relation -
ship between cognition and emotion, this study adopts a 
context-sensitive perspective in which affective responses 
operate as a parallel process whose influence varies 
across contexts. Specifically, AI fear is not treated as a 
simple antecedent to trust-related beliefs, but as an affec -
tive process whose influence varies depending on con -
sent conditions. When consent is unclear or absent, fear 
may heighten moral resistance and suppress acceptance. 
Conversely, when explicit consent is present, fear may 
be reinterpreted or attenuated, resulting in different pat -
terns of association with acceptance.
This conceptualization aligns with dual-process mod -
els of decision-making and human–AI interaction, which 
suggest that cognitive and emotional processes jointly, 
though not uniformly, shape judgments under varying 
contextual conditions. Accordingly, the present study 
examines how cognitive trust-related factors and affec -
tive responses operate simultaneously, while allowing 
their relative influence to differ across consent-based 
scenarios.
The present study
Building on these theoretical and contextual foundations, 
the present study aims to explore the psychological and 
contextual factors that shape college students’ acceptance 
of AI resurrection technologies in China. Accordingly, 
the Chinese cultural context is considered as an interpre-
tive background that informs participants’ meaning-mak-
ing processes, rather than as a formally operationalized 
predictor in the analytical models.
Instead of testing predefined hypotheses, this research 
adopts an exploratory analytic approach guided by the 
Technology Acceptance Model [ 8] and the Human-AI 
Trust Framework [13].
Specifically, we examine how cognitive factors (AI 
engagement and AI harm predictability), affective fac -
tors (AI fear), and contextual or personal characteristics 
(demographics and death-related experiences) relate to 
individuals’ acceptance of AI resurrection across multiple 
hypothetical scenarios that differ in consent conditions 
[30].
Given the exploratory nature of this study and the 
ethical sensitivity of AI resurrection, we did not assume 
that the same psychological mechanisms would operate 
uniformly across all consent scenarios. Instead, the sce -
nario-based analytical strategy was designed to examine 
whether cognitive and affective predictors of acceptance 
remain consistent or vary depending on consent context.
By employing multinomial logistic regression mod -
els across multiple scenarios, this study explicitly allows 
for the possibility that different consent conditions (e.g., 
unclear consent, parental consent, or self-consent) may 
activate distinct psychological processes. This approach

enhances transparency by aligning the research questions 
with the analytical strategy and enables a more nuanced 
interpretation of scenario-dependent findings.
Accordingly, the study addresses the following research 
questions:
1. Which demographic and death-related factors are 
associated with acceptance of AI resurrection across 
consent scenarios?
2. How do cognitive (trust–related) and affective 
(fear–related) dimensions influence acceptance when 
considered simultaneously?
3. Do these associations differ across consent–based 
contextual scenarios (e.g., unknown consent, 
parental consent, or self–consent)?
This is the first empirical study to examine AI resurrec -
tion acceptance in a Chinese cultural context. By focus -
ing on young adults in a rapidly digitizing society, this 
research provides empirical evidence for understanding 
the psychological mechanisms underlying public accep -
tance of AI technologies that intersect with death, auton -
omy, and identity.
Method
Study design and setting
This study aimed to investigate the psychological and 
contextual factors influencing Chinese college students’ 
acceptance of AI resurrection technologies across five 
scenarios. This study employed a cross–sectional design 
to collect data from Chinese college students. Conve -
nience sampling was used to recruit participants from 
seven geographical regions across China, covering 
East, North, Central, South, Southwest, Northwest, and 
Northeast China, to improve geographic diversity. Par -
ticipants were recruited through online distribution of 
the questionnaire link via class-based instant messaging 
groups across institutions in different regions, with the 
assistance of student gatekeepers. Eligible participants 
were current associate, undergraduate, master’s, or doc -
toral students.
The sample was relatively homogeneous in terms of 
age, with 96% of participants concentrated in the 17–29 
years range, and over half were medical students. These 
factors should be considered when interpreting the gen -
eralizability of the findings, as the sample may not fully 
reflect the broader Chinese population. While these 
characteristics align with the theoretical focus of the 
study on young, highly educated individuals, the results 
should not be overgeneralized to the wider population.
Data were collected through a self–designed online 
questionnaire distributed via “Wenjuanxing, ” a one-stop 
survey research platform.
In particular, the relatively high proportion of medical 
students is of theoretical relevance, as medical education 
often involves repeated exposure to mortality, end-of-life 
decision-making, and ethical reflection. These factors are 
likely to influence attitudes toward both AI technologies 
and end-of-life care. Thus, academic major was treated as 
a key analytical variable, enabling us to examine whether 
exposure to medical education is associated with differ -
ential acceptance of AI resurrection technologies.
To ensure an adequate final sample after data quality 
screening, a total of 1002 questionnaires were distrib -
uted. After excluding ineligible or invalid responses, 990 
valid cases were retained for analysis, yielding an effec -
tive response rate of 98.8%.
Sample adequacy was evaluated based on contempo -
rary statistical considerations rather than simple rules of 
thumb. Given the large sample size (N = 990) and the use 
of scenario-specific multinomial logistic regression mod -
els, the study meets commonly recommended criteria for 
model stability and reliable parameter estimation, includ-
ing sufficient observations per outcome category and 
per estimated parameter. Methodological guidelines for 
logistic and multinomial logistic regression commonly 
recommend at least 10–20 observations per estimated 
parameter within each outcome category to ensure sta -
ble model estimation [ 31, 32]. Accordingly, the current 
sample size is considered sufficient to support reliable 
estimation and interpretation of scenario-specific effects.
Prior to participation, respondents read an informed 
consent statement describing the study’s purpose, volun -
tary nature, confidentiality, and right to withdraw at any 
time. Duplicate submissions and responses completed in 
under two minutes were excluded to ensure data quality. 
On average, participants took 10–15 min to complete the 
survey.
The survey comprised five sections: [1] demographic 
information [2], death-related beliefs and experiences [3], 
knowledge and attitudes toward AI [4], five hypothetical 
AI resurrection scenarios and related questions, and [5] 
an ethical question on whether the rights of the deceased 
should be considered in AI resurrection.
While the study uses single-item measures for AI 
harm predictability and AI fear, we acknowledge that 
single-item measures have limitations in terms of con -
struct validity and reliability. These measures were cho -
sen for their simplicity and efficiency in an exploratory 
study with a large sample size. However, we recognize 
that single-item measures may capture a limited scope 
of the construct and could be subject to measurement 
error. Future studies should aim to use multi-item vali -
dated scales to provide more reliable and comprehensive 
assessments of AI-related fears and harm predictability.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethi -
cal principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was 
approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya School 
of Nursing, Central South University. All data were ano -
nymized and securely stored in encrypted files accessible

Survey measures
The questionnaire was designed based on the Technology 
Acceptance Model (TAM) [ 8] and the Human–AI Trust 
Framework [ 13], incorporating vignette-based adapta -
tions from Iwasaki [ 30] to capture ethical and emotional 
responses. It consisted of five major sections: [1] sociode-
mographic characteristics [ 2], death-related beliefs and 
experiences [ 3], AI engagement [ 4], AI harm predict -
ability, and [5] Acceptance of AI resurrection across five 
hypothetical scenarios.
The questionnaire was pretested with five graduate 
students and refined for clarity and reliability. The final 
version achieved a Cronbach’s α of 0.799. All items used 
a 5-point Likert scale unless otherwise stated. The full 
wording of the items and response options is available in 
the Appendix.
Sociodemographic characteristics
Participants reported gender, age, educational level 
(associate, undergraduate, master’s, or doctoral degree), 
and academic major. Majors were subsequently grouped 
into medical and non-medical disciplines for analysis, as 
prior studies have shown that medical students often dis-
play greater exposure to ethical dilemmas, death-related 
experiences, and technology–assisted care, which may 
shape their attitudes toward both AI and end-of-life tech-
nologies [10].
Death-related beliefs and experiences
Two closed–ended items measured participants’ death-
related beliefs and personal experiences related to death. 
The first item assessed afterlife belief: “Do you believe 
death represents the end of life, or do you believe in 
an afterlife or supernatural continuation (e.g., soul or 
spirit)?” (response options: “End of life” or “ Afterlife/spirit 
exists”). Previous research has shown that belief in a soul 
or afterlife is negatively associated with moral approval of 
digital immortality technologies such as mind–upload -
ing [ 33]. The second item asked whether participants 
had experienced the loss of a close family member or 
friend (“Yes” or “No”). Prior work with bereaved popu -
lations demonstrates that such experiences influence 
grief-related digital behaviours and attitudes toward 
posthumous communication technologies [ 34]. These 
two items capture participants’ death-related belief ori -
entation and bereavement experience, which may shape 
their moral evaluation of AI resurrection technologies.
AI engagement
Grounded in the Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) dimen -
sion of the Technology Acceptance Model [8], AI engage-
ment captured participants’ cognitive familiarity and 
experiential interaction with AI systems, which are 
known to facilitate perceived ease and reduce techno -
logical uncertainty [ 14, 21]. Participants indicated [ 1] 
how frequently they used AI technologies (e.g., chatbots, 
generative AI tools, or voice assistants) and [ 2] how well 
they understood these technologies, both measured on a 
5–point Likert scale (1 = “Not at all” to 5 = “Very much”). 
The mean of the two items represented participants’ level 
of AI engagement, with higher scores reflecting greater 
familiarity and perceived ease of interaction.
AI harm predictability
Based on the Human-AI Trust Framework [13], this con-
struct represents a cognitive antecedent of trust, cap -
turing perceived controllability and transparency of AI 
behaviour. Participants were asked, “To what extent do 
you believe the potential harm caused by AI technologies 
can be predicted and managed?” Responses were rated 
on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = “Not at all predictable” to 5 
= “Highly predictable”). Higher scores indicated stronger 
beliefs that AI operates in a controllable and transparent 
manner, aligned with human values and social norms.
Perceived predictability has been shown to enhance 
users’ cognitive trust and willingness to accept AI-medi -
ated systems [13, 18, 35].
AI fear
Drawing on the affective extensions of the Technol -
ogy Acceptance Model [ 9, 21] and the Human–AI Trust 
Framework [13], AI fear represents an affective concern 
about the potential misuse and ethical risks of AI tech -
nologies, reflecting emotional unease toward AI in mor -
ally sensitive contexts.
Participants rated their agreement with the statement: 
“I am concerned that AI technologies, including AI res -
urrection, could be misused or cause ethical problems. ” 
Responses were recorded on a 5–point Likert scale (1 = 
“Strongly disagree” to 5 = “Strongly agree”).
Higher scores indicate stronger emotional unease, 
reflecting fear of ethical misuse, loss of control, or iden -
tity threat associated with AI systems. Prior studies have 
shown that such affective responses negatively predict 
acceptance and trust in AI-driven technologies [ 20, 21, 
26].
AI resurrection scenarios and acceptance
To examine social and personal acceptance of AI resur -
rection, five hypothetical scenarios were adapted from 
Iwasaki [ 30]. Each scenario described a case in which a 
young woman, Xiaotong, was digitally resurrected using

AI technology under varying consent conditions (e.g., 
unknown wishes, parental consent, self-consent). Par -
ticipants rated the social acceptability of each case on a 
5-point Likert scale (1 = “Unacceptable” to 5 = “ Accept -
able”). Two additional vignettes asked participants to 
imagine themselves as (a) the deceased and (b) the 
deceased’s parent, rating their agreement with statements 
regarding their own or their child’s resurrection. The 
final question assessed perceived ethical rights, asking: 
“Whose wishes should be prioritized in AI resurrection? 
the deceased, the family, or both?”
Data analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS ver -
sion 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical 
significance was considered at p < .05 (two–tailed). A 
two-stage analytical strategy was employed.
First, descriptive statistics were computed for all 
variables, expressed as mean (SD) for continuous vari -
ables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical 
variables.
Second, Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were per-
formed to examine bivariate associations among demo -
graphic, death-related, and AI-related variables and 
participants’ acceptance of AI resurrection across the five 
hypothetical scenarios. Variables that showed significant 
correlations (p < .05) were subsequently included in mul -
tinomial logistic regression models to identify predictors 
of AI resurrection acceptance in each scenario.
For demographic factors, three independent vari -
ables were analysed: gender, education, and academic 
major. Gender was included as a binary categorical vari -
able. Academic major was dichotomized into medical 
and non-medical fields. Educational level was grouped 
into three categories (associate, undergraduate, and 
postgraduate).
For death-related beliefs and experiences, two dichoto -
mous variables were included: [1] belief orientation (“end 
of life” vs. “afterlife/spirit exists”) and [2] prior experience 
of losing a close person (“yes” vs. “no”).
For AI-related attitudes, three continuous variables 
were analysed: AI engagement, AI harm predictability, 
and AI fear. In the regression models, continuous vari -
ables were categorized into three levels to facilitate inter -
pretation: low ([1, 2]), moderate [3], and high ([4, 5]). 
Education level was also grouped into three categories 
(associate, undergraduate, postgraduate).
This categorization approach is consistent with prior 
exploratory research on ethically sensitive and posthu -
mous technologies, such as Iwasaki’s study on digital 
cloning, and was adopted to facilitate scenario-based 
interpretation, with results interpreted as indicative 
rather than exact effect estimates [30].
Result
A total of 1002 participants were approached, of whom 
12 were excluded due to ineligibility. The final sample 
comprised 990 valid responses, yielding an effective 
response rate of 98.8%. Descriptive, correlational, and 
regression analyses were performed to examine associa -
tions between demographic, cognitive, and affective vari -
ables and acceptance of AI resurrection.

most were aged between 17 and 29 years (96.0%). The 
largest proportion was undergraduate students (74.5%),

sample.

Overall, the results indicate relatively high accep -
tance of AI resurrection within this student sample, 
with AI-related factors playing a central role in shaping 
acceptance. Higher levels of AI engagement were asso -
ciated with greater acceptance, whereas lower perceived 
predictability of AI-related harm was associated with 
reduced acceptance. Importantly, the influence of AI fear 
was not uniform but varied across consent conditions, 
suggesting that emotional responses to AI resurrection 
are context-dependent. In addition, medical students and 
participants who had experienced the death of a loved 
one tended to report higher acceptance. Together, these 
findings highlight the central role of consent-related con-
ditions in shaping ethical evaluations of AI resurrection.
Acceptance patterns across self- and parent-perspective 
scenarios
In this sample, a majority of participants did not oppose 
the idea of being resurrected through AI (69.1%). This 
proportion appears higher than that reported in earlier 
vignette-based work [ 30], although direct comparisons 
should be made cautiously given differences in samples 
and scenario framing. However, when students imagined 
themselves as parents deciding whether to resurrect their 
child, disagreement increased slightly, suggesting that 
attitudes vary depending on emotional involvement and 
moral standpoint. Such variation highlights the impor -
tance of clarifying ethical and legal responsibilities in 
cases where consent is uncertain or unavailable.
In hypothetical cases where an individual dies unex -
pectedly without surviving relatives, questions arise 
regarding who holds the authority to decide whether 
digital resurrection should occur. Such ethical dilemmas 
underscore the necessity of establishing clear consent 
procedures and robust data governance frameworks to 
prevent posthumous digital reanimation without explicit 
authorization.
AI engagement and acceptance
Across all scenarios, AI engagement was positively asso -
ciated with acceptance of AI resurrection. Students who 
reported greater familiarity with and more frequent use 
of AI systems tended to evaluate AI resurrection more 
favourably. This pattern aligns with prior research indi -
cating that greater exposure to AI is related to higher 
technological readiness and more favourable evalua -
tions of emerging technologies [ 23]. In ethically sensi -
tive applications such as AI resurrection, higher levels of 
engagement may be associated with reduced uncertainty 
and greater psychological comfort when imagining inter -
action with a posthumous digital entity. These findings 
suggest that AI literacy efforts should not focus solely on 
technical competence, but also address how such systems 
function and what their ethical boundaries are in death-
related contexts, including how consent and data gover -
nance are operationalized in posthumous contexts.
Perceived harm predictability and acceptance
Perceived harm predictability showed a consistent asso -
ciation with acceptance of AI resurrection across sce -
narios. Participants who perceived AI behaviour as 
unpredictable or uncontrollable tended to express lower 
acceptance, underscoring the importance of trust-
related appraisals when AI operates in morally sensitive 
domains. This finding reinforces the notion that per -
ceived transparency and controllability are critical for 
building trust in AI [24, 25]. In line with Human–AI trust 
models, transparency in algorithmic processes enables 
users to form cognitive trust by understanding how deci -
sions are made [ 13]. Ensuring that AI resurrection sys -
tems operate in a transparent and predictable manner 
will be crucial for promoting both psychological comfort 
and social acceptance.
From a psychological perspective, this finding can be 
interpreted through the lens of trust-calibration theory 
and risk perception models. When individuals perceive 
AI systems as unpredictable, their sense of control and 
cognitive safety decreases, which may be associated with 
avoidance tendencies. According to dual-process theo -
ries of risk perception [ 36], emotional intuition often 
overrides rational evaluation under uncertainty, thereby 
relating to lower acceptance even when potential bene -
fits are acknowledged. In the context of AI resurrection, 
unpredictability not only threatens technological trust 
but also amplifies existential concerns, as individuals may 
fear that the recreated entity could act beyond moral or 
emotional boundaries.
AI fear level shows opposite effects in different scenarios
AI fear demonstrated contrasting associations with 
acceptance depending on the consent context. When 
consent was absent or unclear, higher levels of AI fear 
were associated with lower acceptance. This pattern is 
consistent with prior findings suggesting that negative 
affect toward AI is linked to avoidance-oriented evalu -
ations under conditions of uncertainty [ 10, 37]. In con -
texts where the deceased’s wishes are ambiguous, AI fear 
may be associated with heightened moral uncertainty 
and existential unease, reflecting an inhibiting affective 
response.
Conversely, under explicit consent conditions, the 
direction of association appeared to reverse, with higher 
AI fear aligning with higher acceptance. These interpre -
tations are necessarily post-hoc, given the exploratory 
design of the study. One possible explanation is that

explicit consent reduces moral ambiguity and redistrib -
utes perceived responsibility, thereby enhancing indi -
viduals’ sense of legitimacy and control. Prior research 
in decision-making has shown that individuals adopt dif -
ferent cognitive and moral reasoning strategies depend -
ing on their role [ 38, 39]. Participants may have felt less 
personal control when consent had already been given, 
which may be associated with ambivalence toward the act 
itself. Under such conditions, fear may heighten aware -
ness of the ethical gravity of AI resurrection without trig-
gering avoidance, instead motivating engagement within 
a morally sanctioned framework. These findings suggest 
that acceptance of AI resurrection is not only determined 
by technology-related factors but also by context-depen -
dent emotional and moral processes.
Importantly, the AI fear measure used in this study may 
capture multiple latent concerns rather than a single, uni-
form construct. In addition to general anxiety about AI 
technologies, such as fears of misuse or loss of control, AI 
fear in the context of resurrection may also reflect moral 
and existential unease related to posthumous identity, 
dignity, and the boundaries between life and death. These 
different dimensions of fear are likely to be differentially 
activated depending on consent clarity, which may help 
explain the observed scenario-dependent effects. Future 
research should disentangle these components using 
multi-item measures that distinguish technological anxi -
ety from existential or moral threat.
Taken together, these findings suggest that the influ -
ence of AI fear on acceptance is not uniform but con -
tingent on the consent context, supporting the role of 
consent scenario as a key boundary condition in evalua -
tions of AI resurrection.
From a theoretical perspective, this pattern is broadly 
compatible with psychological accounts emphasizing 
mortality salience and existential anxiety, such as those 
proposed in Terror Management Theory [ 40]. Rather 
than serving as a primary explanatory framework, these 
perspectives provide a contextual lens for understanding 
why emotional responses to death-related technologies 
may be buffered or intensified depending on perceived 
moral authorization and control. Accordingly, AI fear 
should not be conceptualized as a uniformly inhibit -
ing factor, as often assumed in conventional technology 
acceptance research, but as a context-sensitive affective 
response whose psychological meaning varies with con -
sent conditions.
Although these associations were statistically reliable, 
most effects were small to moderate in magnitude, sug -
gesting that acceptance of AI resurrection is likely shaped 
by multiple additional moral, contextual, and situational 
factors beyond the predictors measured in the present 
study.
Other factors related to students’ acceptance
Disciplinary background and life experience were also 
associated with acceptance. Medical students demon -
strated higher acceptance than non–medical students, 
which may be associated with their greater exposure to 
mortality and ethical reflection through academic and 
clinical training [41]. Similarly, students who had experi -
enced the death of a loved one were more accepting of AI 
resurrection. This pattern echoes earlier work suggesting 
that bereavement increases sensitivity to death-related 
issues and may heighten motivation to reconnect sym -
bolically with the deceased [42].
The deceased’s consent is indispensable for individuals’ AI 
resurrection
The deceased’s consent emerged as a decisive ethical 
factor. Nearly half of the participants (47.9%) believed 
that decisions about AI resurrection should balance the 
wishes of the deceased and their family, while 43.9% 
believed the deceased’s pre–mortem consent should take 
full precedence. Only 6.3% supported decisions made 
solely by the family. Acceptance tended to be higher in 
scenarios involving explicit consent from the deceased 
than in those relying on parental consent.
These findings support Harbinja’s [ 5] argument that 
explicit lifetime consent is a necessary condition for digi -
tal replication or digital cloning. Future ethical and legal 
frameworks should prioritize autonomy, informed con -
sent, and the dignity of the deceased when addressing 
AI–mediated posthumous technologies.
Cultural specificity and cross-cultural generalizability
The present findings should be interpreted within their 
cultural context. In China, Confucian-influenced rela -
tional perspectives on personhood emphasize family 
responsibility, interdependence, and moral continuity 
after death, which may shape attitudes toward posthu -
mous technologies such as AI resurrection [ 43]. These 
cultural values may influence how participants interpret 
consent, dignity, and appropriate boundaries between the 
living and the deceased.
At the same time, several core psychological mecha -
nisms identified in this study, such as trust in AI systems, 
affective responses to AI, and consent-based moral rea -
soning, are not unique to the Chinese context and may be 
relevant across cultures. What may differ cross-culturally 
is the relative salience and expression of these mecha -
nisms rather than their fundamental presence. Future 
cross-cultural research is therefore needed to examine 
the generalizability of these findings and to clarify how 
cultural frameworks interact with psychological pro -
cesses in shaping acceptance of AI-mediated posthumous 
technologies.

Theoretical implications for technology acceptance and 
human–AI interaction
With respect to technology acceptance theory, the pres -
ent findings indicate that applying the Technology Accep-
tance Model (TAM) to existentially sensitive applications 
such as AI resurrection requires reconsideration of its 
core assumptions. TAM-based research typically concep-
tualizes acceptance as an instrumental evaluation driven 
by perceived usefulness and ease of use. In contrast, the 
findings of this study suggest that, in posthumous AI con-
texts, acceptance judgments are fundamentally shaped by 
moral legitimacy and the clarity of consent, in addition to 
cognitive evaluations. Rather than rejecting TAM, these 
results highlight its contextual limitations and suggest 
that cognitive assessments operate alongside ethical and 
affective considerations when AI technologies engage 
with death and identity.
Beyond TAM, this study extends understanding of 
human–AI interaction by demonstrating that trust, fear, 
and consent function differently in existential contexts 
than in conventional AI applications. Trust in AI in the 
context of resurrection is not limited to technical predict-
ability or controllability, but also involves perceived moral 
alignment with human values when AI systems represent 
the deceased. AI fear does not act as a uniformly inhib -
iting factor; instead, its influence varies depending on 
consent conditions, reflecting a context-sensitive affec -
tive response. Consent, which is often treated as a proce -
dural prerequisite in conventional AI systems, emerges in 
this study as a central factor shaping both moral reason -
ing and emotional responses across scenarios. Together, 
these findings clarify how human–AI interaction in exis -
tential domains departs from conventional AI use cases 
and underscore the need for context-aware approaches to 
technology acceptance.
Scenario-level synthesis
Across the five consent scenarios, a consistent pattern 
emerged in which acceptance was lowest when consent 
was unclear, and higher when explicit consent, either 
from the individual prior to death or from close family 
members, was present. Moreover, differences between 
self- and parent-perspective scenarios suggest that accep-
tance judgments are shaped not only by consent status 
but also by role-based moral reasoning, reflecting how 
individuals negotiate responsibility, authority, and emo -
tional proximity in decisions about posthumous digital 
representation.
Limitations
The present study has several limitations that should be 
considered when interpreting the findings.
First, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes 
causal inference. Although significant associations were 
identified between cognitive, affective, and demographic 
variables and acceptance of AI resurrection, the direc -
tionality and causality of these relationships cannot be 
determined from the present data. All findings should 
therefore be interpreted as reflecting correlational pat -
terns rather than causal effects. Longitudinal or experi -
mental designs are needed in future research to establish 
temporal precedence and clarify the causal mechanisms 
underlying acceptance of AI resurrection technologies.
Second, the sample was relatively homogeneous, with 
the majority of participants aged between 17 and 29 years 
and a high proportion of medical students. In addition, 
the sample exhibited limited variability in age and educa -
tional background. These characteristics may restrict the 
generalizability of the findings to broader populations, 
including Chinese society as a whole. Future studies 
should recruit more diverse samples across age groups, 
educational backgrounds, and social contexts to enhance 
external validity.
Third, AI harm predictability and AI fear were assessed 
using single-item measures. Although single-item mea -
sures can be appropriate in exploratory research with 
large samples, they may limit construct precision and 
reliability. Future research should employ validated 
multi-item scales to more comprehensively capture 
different dimensions of AI-related concerns, includ -
ing distinctions between general technological anxiety 
and moral or existential unease related to posthumous 
technologies.
Fourth, several potentially relevant psychologi -
cal variables were not measured in the present study, 
such as death anxiety and prior exposure to AI resur -
rection technologies or related narratives. These fac -
tors may influence individuals’ emotional responses and 
ethical judgments regarding AI resurrection. Future 
research should incorporate these variables to further 
clarify the psychological mechanisms underlying accep -
tance and resistance toward AI-mediated posthumous 
technologies.
Implications
The findings of this study have important implications for 
education, policy, and future research on ethically sensi -
tive AI applications, particularly those involving death, 
identity, and posthumous data.
Educational implications
The present findings suggest that AI literacy in ethically 
sensitive domains should extend beyond technical com -
petence to include ethical reasoning, consent aware -
ness, and cultural understanding. While prior work often 
frames AI literacy in terms of functional skills, our results 
indicate that acceptance of AI resurrection is closely tied

Cheng et al. BMC Psychology          (2026) 14:794 
to individuals’ cognitive familiarity with AI as well as 
their ethical and emotional evaluations.
However, prior research has shown that most col -
lege students do not receive structured AI instruction 
[22], and a U.S. survey reported that about half of stu -
dents were concerned about AI’s influence on education, 
while 60% indicated that their institutions had not clearly 
defined ethical guidelines for AI use [ 44]. Although these 
figures come from a U.S. context, they illustrate that ethi-
cal guidance often lags behind rapid adoption of genera -
tive AI in higher education. Accordingly, educational 
programs should emphasize three specific components of 
AI literacy that are particularly relevant for death-related 
AI applications.
First, ethical awareness should be strengthened by 
engaging students in structured discussions about moral 
dilemmas raised by posthumous technologies, including 
autonomy, dignity, identity continuity, and psychological 
harm. Scenario-based learning, similar to the vignettes 
used in this study, may be especially effective for foster -
ing ethical reflection in emotionally complex contexts.
Second, informed consent literacy should be explic -
itly addressed. Students should be trained to understand 
consent not only as a legal requirement but as an ongo -
ing ethical process, particularly when AI systems operate 
beyond the lifespan of the data subject. This includes rec -
ognizing the limits of proxy decision-making, the role of 
prior expressed wishes, and the long-term implications of 
posthumous data use.
Third, cultural sensitivity should be incorporated into 
AI education. Given that attitudes toward death, per -
sonhood, and family responsibility vary across cultural 
contexts, AI literacy programs should help students rec -
ognize how cultural frameworks shape ethical judgments. 
This is particularly important for AI systems intended for 
global deployment, where culturally insensitive design 
may undermine public trust and acceptance.
Ethical and policy implications
From a policy perspective, the findings underscore the 
need for context-specific consent frameworks for ethi -
cally sensitive AI applications such as AI resurrection. 
Unlike conventional AI systems, posthumous technolo -
gies raise unique challenges regarding autonomy, moral 
responsibility, and the authority to decide on behalf of 
the deceased.
Many participants favored prioritizing the deceased’s 
wishes or balancing them with family involvement, 
underscoring both strong support for lifetime consent 
and the perceived ethical relevance of relational decision-
making. This pattern highlights both strong support for 
lifetime consent and the perceived ethical relevance of 
family involvement in posthumous decision-making.
Taken together, these findings suggest that explicit 
lifetime consent should be treated as a primary ethi -
cal safeguard for AI resurrection, while also acknowl -
edging the relational dimensions of decision-making in 
death-related contexts. Regulatory frameworks should 
therefore require clear, informed, and revocable consent 
mechanisms established during an individual’s lifetime, 
specifying the scope, duration, and permissible uses of 
posthumous digital representations.
At the same time, consent frameworks should remain 
flexible enough to account for cultural and relational dif -
ferences. In societies where family responsibility plays a 
central moral role, policy designs may need to balance 
individual autonomy with family participation, while still 
prioritizing the expressed wishes of the deceased. Avoid -
ing one-size-fits-all consent models and adopting cultur -
ally adaptive governance approaches may help reduce 
ethical conflict, perceived uncertainty, and public resis -
tance to posthumous AI technologies.
Research implications
This study also points to several directions for future 
research. First, given the exploratory nature of the pres -
ent work, future studies should employ longitudinal, 
qualitative, or mixed-methods designs to examine how 
attitudes toward AI resurrection evolve over time and 
across different life stages or experiences with loss.
Second, future research should refine the measurement 
of AI-related affect by distinguishing between differ -
ent dimensions of AI fear, such as technological anxiety, 
moral unease, and existential concern related to iden -
tity and dignity. Using validated multi-item scales would 
allow for a more precise examination of how emotional 
responses interact with trust and consent judgments in 
ethically sensitive AI contexts.
Finally, as suggested by Nawrat [ 45], advancing AI 
research can enhance our understanding of how to 
design, validate, and integrate socially responsible AI 
solutions. This includes improving consent frameworks, 
ensuring transparency, and addressing ethical concerns 
in AI applications, particularly those involving human 
identity and posthumous technologies. Comparative 
cross-cultural research is also needed to investigate 
how consent preferences, trust calibration, and affective 
responses to AI resurrection vary across societies with 
different cultural views on death, autonomy, and family 
responsibility. Such work would help clarify which psy -
chological mechanisms identified in this study are cultur-
ally specific and which may generalize across contexts, 
thereby informing the responsible global governance of 
posthumous AI technologies.

Conclusions
This study contributes empirical evidence on the psy -
chological mechanisms and contextual factors shaping 
acceptance of AI resurrection among Chinese college 
students. By integrating cognitive (AI engagement, harm 
predictability) and affective (AI fear) dimensions within 
a moral framework, the study highlights the interplay 
between technological trust and ethical awareness. The 
results emphasize that consent, transparency, and famil -
iarity are key correlates of acceptance within this sample 
of public acceptance. Future research should further 
explore emotional and cultural factors through qualita -
tive or cross–cultural approaches to support the ethical 
governance of AI resurrection and related technologies.
